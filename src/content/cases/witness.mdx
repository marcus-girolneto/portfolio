## The engineering problem

An append-only API is a promise made by its operator. A transparency log makes that promise inspectable: clients can verify that an entry belongs to a committed tree and that a newer tree extends an older one.

Witness is an independent Go project implementing RFC 6962 Merkle inclusion and consistency proofs, Ed25519 signed checkpoints, crash-safe file storage and a split-view monitor.

## From an entry to a verifiable receipt

A single sequencer batches queued appends, writes the entry file and hash index, synchronizes storage, computes the new Merkle root and atomically persists a signed checkpoint. The request receives its receipt only after the entry is durable and covered by that checkpoint.

Clients verify both checkpoint signatures and Merkle proofs. The server’s statement is evidence to inspect, rather than a reason to bypass verification. Historical subtree hashes allow proofs to be assembled without rehashing the entire log.

## Why domain separation matters

RFC 6962 hashes leaves with a `0x00` prefix and internal nodes with a `0x01` prefix. Those prefixes keep leaf data and internal hash concatenations in different domains. Without the separation, a tree node could be confused with an entry containing its child hashes.

## Durability and conflicting histories

The file format includes checksums. Startup reconstructs and verifies state against the last signed checkpoint, refusing a store that has become shorter or different. A failed synchronization poisons further writes rather than allowing the service to claim durability it cannot establish.

A signature alone does not prevent a dishonest operator from signing two histories. The monitor persists a trusted checkpoint and checks subsequent views for consistency, rollback, forks and conflicting equal-size roots. Split-view detection depends on the views meeting; an isolated client cannot infer what another audience was shown.

## Tradeoffs and limits

Group commit amortizes synchronization across a batch and a single writer simplifies ordering. The current design verifies the log at startup, making recovery work proportional to its size. File-backed storage and an in-memory offset index are deliberate limits of this compact implementation.

Witness cosigning, freshness policies and tiled storage are documented roadmap items, not capabilities claimed as shipped here. The linked repository explains those limits and the differential, fuzz, storage-recovery and monitor tests. Transparency requires both sound cryptographic proofs and a plan for who observes them.
